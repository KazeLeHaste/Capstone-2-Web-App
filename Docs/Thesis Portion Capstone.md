## INTRODUCTION 

Traffic congestion remains one of the most critical challenges affecting urban mobility in rapidly developing cities across the Philippines. Bacoor City, located in the province of Cavite, exemplifies this problem as its growing population and economic expansion place increasing pressure on existing road infrastructure. Limited road capacity, uncoordinated traffic signals, and the absence of data-driven planning tools contribute to severe bottlenecks during peak hours, resulting in extended travel times, elevated fuel consumption, increased vehicle emissions, and reduced productivity. 



Current traffic management practices in Bacoor rely primarily on manual enforcement and conventional fixed-timer signal systems. While these methods provide basic traffic control, they lack the flexibility and analytical capability needed to respond dynamically to fluctuating traffic conditions or to evaluate the effectiveness of proposed interventions before implementation. Traffic authorities face significant challenges in justifying infrastructure investments and policy changes without robust evidence derived from simulation and scenario analysis. 



This capstone project, "Traffic Simulator: Analysis and Recommendation of Effect in Traffic Management Strategies," aims to address these challenges by developing a simulation system focused on modeling real-world traffic conditions and providing actionable analytics. The simulator 





**2 **





is designed to help stakeholders—such as traffic authorities, city planners, and researchers—evaluate and compare the effectiveness of diverse traffic management strategies. By generating data-driven recommendations and visualizing complex traffic scenarios, the project seeks to support more informed decision-making and improve traffic flow in local urban settings. 



**1.1 Problem and its Background **

Bacoor City's traffic congestion stems from multiple interconnected factors. Rapid urbanization has led to a substantial increase in vehicle ownership without corresponding expansion of road infrastructure. Key arterial roads and major intersections frequently experience gridlock during morning and evening rush hours, affecting not only commuters but also commercial transport and emergency services. The absence of real-time traffic monitoring systems means that traffic management decisions are often reactive rather than proactive, addressing problems only after they have significantly impacted mobility. 



Traditional approaches to traffic management—such as deploying additional traffic enforcers or adjusting signal timings based on observation—provide limited insight into the systemic effects of interventions. Without simulation and modeling tools, traffic planners cannot accurately predict how changes in signal timing, road configurations, or enforcement strategies will influence overall traffic flow. This limitation hinders evidence-based policymaking and infrastructure investment decisions, resulting in suboptimal resource allocation and continued congestion. 





**3 **





Moreover, the cost and complexity of real-world traffic experiments make it impractical to test multiple strategies simultaneously. Implementing changes to physical infrastructure or signal systems requires significant time and financial investment, and unsuccessful interventions can exacerbate congestion rather than alleviate it. There is thus a critical need for a simulation-based decision-support tool that enables traffic authorities to evaluate potential solutions in a virtual environment before committing resources to implementation. 

**1.1.1 Project Context **

Traffic simulation has emerged as a vital tool for urban planners and transportation engineers worldwide. Advanced simulation platforms like SUMO \(Simulation of Urban MObility\) provide the technical capability to model complex traffic scenarios with high fidelity, incorporating factors such as vehicle behavior, signal timing, road geometry, and traffic volume. However, these tools often require specialized technical knowledge, making them inaccessible to local government units and traffic management departments that lack dedicated simulation expertise. 



In the Philippine context, there is an urgent need for localized, accessible simulation and analytics tools. By focusing on analytics and actionable recommendations, this capstone project aligns with current trends in smart mobility and urban data-driven governance. The proposed traffic simulator for Bacoor City will allow users to explore and compare different strategies, visualize their impacts, and generate evidence-based recommendations for 





**4 **





improving urban mobility. This project serves as both a technical innovation and an educational resource, supporting the broader theme of "Games and Society – Social Impact Games."

**1.3 Scope and Limitations **

**1.3.1 Scope of the Study **

This study focuses on developing a web-based platform that integrates SUMO traffic simulation capabilities with an accessible user interface designed for traffic management professionals in Bacoor City. The system architecture consists of a Python Flask backend with SQLite database, a React frontend with real-time WebSocket communication, and SUMO as the core simulation engine. 

The platform provides users with a configuration interface to set simulation parameters before selecting from 7 pre-configured traffic networks representing key areas in Bacoor City. 

These networks were imported from OpenStreetMap using SUMO's OSM Web Wizard and include realistic road layouts, traffic signals, and vehicle flow data calibrated to local conditions. 

Traffic scenarios focus on vehicular flows across commonly encountered road configurations. SUMO's built-in behavior models—including the sublane model for realistic lane-changing—simulate vehicle interactions, signal responses, and congestion patterns. The system does not implement custom traffic algorithms but rather leverages SUMO's established 





**7 **





microscopic simulation capabilities. 

Simulation outputs \(e.g., average waiting time, queue length, vehicle throughput, congestion levels, emissions data\) are collected through SUMO's output files and presented through an analytics dashboard featuring charts, graphs, and KPI summaries. The primary goal is to facilitate data-driven analysis and recommendations for traffic management in Bacoor City. 

The system supports multiple concurrent simulation sessions through enhanced session management, with each session receiving dedicated resources, isolated temporary directories, and dynamic TraCI port allocation. A comprehensive SQLite database stores session configuration, real-time metrics, trip data, time series information, and generated recommendations for historical analysis and comparative evaluation. 



**1.3.2 Limitation of the Study **

The study is subject to several limitations inherent to its design and implementation: 

• **Network Coverage:** The simulator includes 7 select road networks representing key congestion points in Bacoor City area. It does not model the entire city road system. 

Network expansion requires importing additional scenarios using SUMO's OSM Web Wizard and the included OSM scenario importer tool. 

• **Pre-Configured Networks**: Users select from existing traffic scenarios rather than building custom road layouts. The system does not include a drag-and-drop road 





**8 **





builder interface. Networks are created externally using SUMO tools and imported into the application. 

• **Simulation Engine:** The system uses SUMO as the core simulation engine rather than implementing custom traffic simulation algorithms. Vehicle behavior models \(car-following, lane-changing\) are provided by SUMO's built-in capabilities including the sublane model, not custom IDM/MOBIL implementations. 

• **Simulation Data:** All scenarios are based on pre-configured parameters derived from OpenStreetMap data and local traffic observations rather than live, real-time data feeds. The tool does not integrate continuous sensor data or live traffic information. 

• **Traffic Control Logic:** Adaptive traffic control uses SUMO's native actuated traffic light logic and netconvert-based traffic light addition to priority intersections. The system does not implement advanced machine learning approaches such as deep learning or reinforcement learning for traffic optimization. 

• **Visualization:** Real-time traffic visualization is provided by SUMO's GUI application. The web interface provides configuration, monitoring, and analytics capabilities but does not render real-time vehicle movements in the browser. 

• **Scope of Control Methods:** The application models signalized intersections with various control strategies \(existing signals, fixed-timer, adaptive, buhos/counterflow, custom timing\). Other forms of traffic management, such as emergency vehicle prioritization, incident response, or weather-related effects, have limited implementation. 





**9 **





• **User Configuration:** The system provides essential simulation parameters through the configuration interface while maintaining accessibility for non-expert users. 

Advanced SUMO configuration options are not exposed through the web interface. 

• **Intended Use:** The web application is intended for simulation, educational, research, and decision-support purposes only. It is not designed for direct deployment or operational integration into Bacoor City's live traffic management systems. 

• **Desktop Application Architecture:** The system operates as a desktop application running on localhost, launching SUMO GUI windows on the local machine. It is not designed for cloud deployment or remote multi-user access in its current form. 

• **Feedback and Future Development:** While feedback from stakeholders is encouraged to guide future improvements, the current version remains a prototype and is not production-ready. 

In summary, the traffic simulator provides a practical web-based platform for configuring, executing, and analyzing SUMO traffic simulations for selected areas of Bacoor City, with a focus on usability and actionable analytics. Its scope and limitations reflect the project's commitment to bridging the gap between advanced traffic simulation capabilities and the needs of local decision-makers and non-expert users.

**1.5.1 Simulation-Based Traffic Modeling **

Traffic simulation offers a computational method to visualize and predict traffic behavior under varying conditions. Mostafi et al. \(2022\) developed RegTraffic, a regression-based simulator capable of visualizing spatiotemporal congestion. However, the system did not allow for testing various control strategies or interventions, which is critical for policy formulation. To address this, the capstone system incorporates simulation modules that analyze different traffic management approaches, allowing localized experimentation in Bacoor City. 

Zhang et al. \(2024\) introduced MOSS, a large-scale traffic simulator using generative models and satellite images to emulate regional traffic demand. However, its broad regional focus overlooked granular variables such as signal control, traffic behavior, or street-level congestion dynamics. The capstone bridges this gap by offering a more detailed and configurable simulation environment focused on specific intersections and congestion zones in Bacoor. 





**14 **





Eden, Taguiam, and Palmiano \(2021\) conducted a validation study using LocalSim to replicate Filipino driving behavior in simulations, acknowledging that localized patterns, such as unpredictable lane switching and spontaneous pedestrian crossings, deviate from Western standards. However, the study lacked a robust analytical component to quantify the impact of these behaviors. This limitation underscores the importance of integrating metrics such as vehicle throughput and travel delay in the capstone system for comprehensive evaluation. 

Jiang et al. \(2024\) advanced the use of multi-GPU environments for real-time city-wide simulation, optimizing data speed and realism. Despite this, the model required high-end infrastructure, making it less accessible for local governments. The proposed capstone addresses this by designing a lightweight, modular simulator deployable on standard systems while maintaining the ability to present complex simulations and generate comparative results. 



**1.5.2 Adaptive Signal Control and Intelligent Systems **

The evolution of traffic signal management from fixed-timer to adaptive systems has been accelerated by AI. A 2025 study on Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning explored the use of decentralized agents to adapt signal patterns in real-time. However, the study’s implementation was heavily reliant on sensor networks and real-time inputs, which are often unavailable in smaller urban centers. The capstone responds to this by simulating adaptive logic through rule-based decision structures in offline environments, making it possible to analyze signal timing strategies under simulated conditions. 





**15 **





Mirbakhsh and Azizi \(2024\) introduced D3QN, an adaptive multi-objective deep reinforcement model capable of adjusting signals based on multiple environmental factors. 

While the model achieved high efficiency in lab environments, it offered limited interpretability for traffic managers. The capstone integrates a visual interface and dashboards, translating simulation data into understandable insights to support non-technical stakeholders in decision-making. 

MoveLight by Shao et al. \(2024\) used deep learning to manage traffic flow at the lane level, reducing queue times and wait durations. However, it lacked customizability for localized simulation or manual testing of scenarios. The capstone expands this capability by allowing users to input and simulate various strategies manually, including lane-specific changes, to observe impacts on queue length and congestion. 

Han et al. \(2024\) introduced CycLight, which coordinated signal cycles across corridors using RL. Although effective, its large-scale synchronization design overlooked smaller city needs. The capstone addresses this by offering both individual and grouped signal testing modules specific to Bacoor, enabling analysis of micro-level optimizations aligned with city-wide planning. 

Local implementations such as the Mandaue City AI Traffic Pilot \(2025\) and Mapúa University’s Smart Signal Control Project \(2021\) show that AI-powered traffic systems can succeed in Philippine contexts. However, both lacked pre-deployment simulation tools for strategy vetting. The capstone simulates these strategies virtually, supporting data-informed decisions and risk-free experimentation. 





**16 **





**1.5.3 Environmental and Societal Impact **

Traffic systems today must consider not only mobility but also sustainability and public well-being. Jones et al. \(2021\) established that idle reduction via smart signal systems could lower fuel consumption. However, the study did not offer a user-accessible tool to visualize or quantify this impact. In contrast, the capstone uses simulation to analyze congestion-related emissions and integrates these metrics into the output dashboard. 

Taylor et al. \(2020\) discussed the broader societal benefits of AI-optimized traffic management, such as improved air quality and reduced stress. Despite these findings, their analysis was theoretical and not tied to specific intervention data. The capstone addresses this by applying practical analytics to real simulation outcomes, giving stakeholders actionable data they can use to prioritize both mobility and environmental goals. 

Tripathi and Sharma \(2024\) used regression models to predict traffic volumes and emissions during peak hours. While predictive, their system lacked visualization for scenario comparison. The capstone builds upon this by providing a graphical comparison interface that allows users to observe how different interventions affect emission trends, delay time, and vehicle accumulation. 



**1.5.4 Policy, Enforcement, and Public Behavior **

Traffic management strategies rely on both technological design and behavioral 





**17 **





compliance. The No-Contact Apprehension Policy \(2022–2023\) improved enforcement using cameras, but public pushback highlighted the lack of transparency and simulation prior to deployment. The capstone directly addresses this gap by providing BTMD with a platform to test new enforcement strategies virtually and visualize how driver behavior might change under different conditions. 

Project MOVE \(MMDA, 2022\) recommended simulation-based testing of urban traffic schemes, yet provided no technical tool for smaller cities to adopt. The capstone builds on this by delivering an accessible simulation tool that local governments like Bacoor can use to evaluate their traffic schemes and improve compliance through data-informed adjustments. 

Garcia and Santos \(2020\) modeled traffic efficiency at roundabouts and found that under specific conditions, roundabouts performed better than signalized intersections.

**2.1. Purpose and Description **

**Content of the Study **

This capstone project develops a web-based platform that integrates SUMO 

\(Simulation of Urban MObility\) traffic simulation capabilities with an accessible user interface, designed specifically for traffic management professionals in Bacoor City. The system enables users to configure simulation parameters, select from pre-configured Philippine traffic scenarios, execute simulations, and analyze results through comprehensive analytics dashboards—all without requiring deep technical knowledge of SUMO. 

The platform architecture consists of three primary components: **1. Backend System \(Flask \+ SQLite\): **A Python Flask web server provides RESTful API endpoints for configuration management, simulation control, and data retrieval. SQLite database stores session information, configuration parameters, real-time metrics, trip data, time series analytics, and AI-generated recommendations. The enhanced session manager 





**24 **





coordinates multiple concurrent simulations with isolated resources and dynamic port allocation. 



**2. Frontend Application \(React\):** A React-based single-page application provides an intuitive user interface with five main pages: Home \(system status\), Configuration \(simulation parameters\), Network Selection \(choosing from 7 Philippine scenarios\), Simulation \(monitoring active runs\), and Analytics \(post-simulation analysis\). Real-time WebSocket communication enables live monitoring of simulation metrics. 



**3. Simulation Engine \(SUMO\):** SUMO performs the actual traffic simulation, modeling individual vehicle movements, traffic signal operations, and interactions based on configured parameters. The system launches SUMO GUI instances for visualization while collecting simulation outputs \(tripinfo.xml, summary.xml, emissions.xml\) for analytics processing. 

The workflow follows a configuration-first approach: users first set simulation parameters \(time duration, traffic scale, vehicle types, control methods\), then select a network from 7 pre-configured Bacoor area scenarios, launch the simulation with SUMO GUI, monitor progress via WebSocket, and finally analyze results through the analytics dashboard which provides KPI summaries, charts, recommendations, and data export capabilities. 

The system leverages SUMO's built-in traffic behavior models—including the sublane model for realistic lane-changing—rather than implementing custom simulation algorithms. 





**25 **





Networks are created using SUMO's OSM Web Wizard tool and imported into the application using the included OSM scenario importer. This approach prioritizes accessibility and practical utility over algorithmic novelty. 

While the simulator currently focuses on vehicular traffic and pre-configured scenarios \(not live data\), its modular architecture allows for future expansion—such as adding new road configurations, multi-modal transport elements, and sensor-based inputs. 

Ultimately, the tool aims to provide BTMD and local stakeholders with simulation-based analytics for smarter, data-driven urban mobility planning. 



**Problems of the Study **

Bacoor City's traffic management faces several interconnected challenges: \(1\) lack of tools for evaluating proposed interventions before implementation, \(2\) reliance on fixed-timer signals that cannot adapt to varying traffic conditions, \(3\) absence of data-driven evidence to justify infrastructure investments, and \(4\) inaccessibility of professional simulation tools \(like SUMO\) to non-technical traffic management personnel. 

Existing approaches—deploying additional enforcers or adjusting signal timings based on observation—provide limited insight into systemic effects and cannot predict outcomes of proposed changes. Real-world experimentation is costly, time-consuming, and risks exacerbating congestion if interventions prove ineffective. 





**26 **

Such a tool would help Bacoor transition from reactive, manual practices to evidence-based, efficient traffic management.

**30 **

The web page \(ConfigurationPage.js\) where users set essential simulation parameters including begin/end time, traffic scale, vehicle types, and control methods before selecting a network and launching simulation.

A traffic performance indicator calculated from SUMO simulation data, representing the buildup of vehicles and reduced speeds over time, especially during high-density or peak-hour conditions.

System design approach where simulation sessions, configurations, results, and analytics are persistently stored in SQLite database, enabling historical analysis, session comparison, and data retrieval. 



**2.1.2.7 Delay Time **

The average time vehicles spend waiting at intersections or in traffic queues, calculated by SUMO and presented in the analytics dashboard to assess traffic efficiency.

Backend component \(enhanced\_session\_manager.py\) that coordinates multiple concurrent SUMO simulation sessions with isolated resources, dynamic port allocation, and session lifecycle management. 



**2.1.2.9 Fixed-Timer Signal Control **

Traditional traffic light system with pre-set timing sequences that do not respond to real-time changes in vehicle volume or road conditions, one of the control methods available in the simulator.

Python web framework \(Flask 2.3.3\) serving as the backend server, providing RESTful API endpoints, WebSocket communication, SUMO integration, and database operations.

Quantitative metrics computed from SUMO simulation outputs including average travel delay, throughput, queue length, average speed, congestion index, fuel consumption, and emissions.

SUMO's modeling approach that simulates individual vehicles' behavior including speed, acceleration, lane-changing, and decision-making at intersections, providing high-fidelity traffic flow representation.

System capability to run multiple SUMO simulations concurrently on the same machine, each with isolated temporary directories, dedicated TraCI ports \(8813\+\), and independent resource allocation.

**2.7.2.2.8 Algorithm Structure **

The Traffic Simulation System adopts a modular orchestration-based algorithmic structure centered on workflow management, data processing, and SUMO integration, rather than custom vehicle behavior models. It leverages the Simulation of Urban MObility \(SUMO\) engine for microscopic traffic computation and focuses on efficient coordination of simulation execution, real-time monitoring, KPI computation, and automated recommendation generation. 

This design ensures reliable, reproducible traffic analysis for Bacoor City and supports flexible experimentation with different management strategies through a web-based platform. 



**2.7.2.2.8.1 Simulation Workflow Orchestration **

The core simulation process follows a workflow-based algorithm that manages the lifecycle of each simulation session. The algorithm performs the following functions: 

• Initializes session data and temporary directories 

• Generates SUMO configuration files based on user-defined parameters and selected network scenarios 



• Launches the SUMO process using the TRACI \(Traffic Control Interface\) protocol for live data exchange 





**75 **





• Executes iterative simulation steps, collecting and broadcasting live traffic metrics in real time 

• Parses SUMO’s output files \(tripinfo, summary, emissions\) upon completion 

• Computes analytics and KPIs from the gathered data and stores them in the system database 

• Sends live updates and final results to the user interface via WebSocket communication This modular workflow ensures process isolation, robust error handling, and efficient data transfer between the backend and the SUMO engine.. 



**2.7.2.2.8.2 Key Performance Indicator \(KPI\) Computation **

Following each simulation, the backend algorithm calculates Key Performance Indicators that quantify the overall traffic efficiency and sustainability Metrics include: 

• **Average Travel Delay** – Derived from total time loss per trip 

• **Throughput** – Number of successfully completed trips 

• **Average Queue Length** – Based on cumulative waiting steps over simulation intervals 

• **Average Speed** – Mean velocity across all vehicles per interval 

• **Congestion Index** – Ratio of time loss to total trip duration 





**76 **





• **Emission Levels** – Aggregated CO₂, NOx, PMx, and fuel consumption from SUMO’s emission reports 

• **Safety Score** – Computed inversely from total collisions and teleports recorded These KPIs form the analytical foundation for generating actionable insights and strategy recommendations.at quantify the overall traffic efficiency and sustainability. 



**2.7.2.2.8.3 Recommendation Generation Engine **

The system integrates a rule-based recommendation algorithm that analyzes KPI results and automatically suggests optimal interventions for congestion management, safety improvement, and environmental sustainability. 

Each recommendation entry includes a type, priority, description, target metric, and expected impact. 

Sample decision rules include: 

• Triggering adaptive signal control when congestion exceeds 30% 

• Suggesting road capacity improvements when average speed drops below 20 km/h 

• Encouraging eco-routing or public transport adoption for high emission outputs 

• Optimizing signal timing when queue lengths become excessive 

• Recommending speed limit reviews for low safety scores 





**77 **





All recommendations are prioritized according to impact severity, ensuring that the most critical interventions are presented first.. 



**2.7.2.2.8.4 Data Management and Communication **

The backend employs asynchronous data handling through WebSocket protocols for continuous real-time communication between the SUMO engine, the Flask server, and the frontend interface. 

During simulation: 

• Live metrics are broadcast to the visualization dashboard 

• Completed data are stored in structured databases for retrieval and reporting 

• Intermediate data \(tripinfo, summary, emissions\) are parsed, transformed, and cached to minimize latency 

This data-centric approach ensures both performance efficiency and transparency throughout the simulation process. 



**2.7.2.2.8.5 SUMO Integration and Control Interface **

The algorithmic structure relies on the TraCI \(Traffic Control Interface\) to control SUMO dynamically during execution. Through this interface, the backend can: 





**78 **





• Trigger simulation steps in real time 

• Retrieve live vehicle, lane, and traffic light data 

• Adjust configurations on-the-fly \(e.g., phase timing, network changes\) 

• Gracefully terminate or resume sessions based on system commands This integration bridges the backend orchestration with SUMO’s robust simulation capabilities, providing a flexible platform for experimentation and analysis. 



**2.7.2.2.8.6 System Intelligence and Extensibility **

While the current implementation utilizes rule-based analytics, the architecture is modular and designed for future integration of AI-driven optimization modules, such as: 

• Reinforcement learning for adaptive signal timing 

• Predictive analytics for congestion forecasting 

• Automated calibration of traffic parameters using machine learning This forward-compatible structure ensures long-term scalability and adaptability for research and urban planning applications..

**4.3 Implementation Plan **

This section outlines a practical plan for deploying the Traffic Simulator in a pilot operational environment and preparing stakeholders to use it effectively. 

**4.3.1 Objective **

To pilot the Traffic Simulator within a traffic management department for short-term validation and training use. 





**198 **





**4.3.2 Scope & timeline **

• Weeks 0–2: Deployment and configuration 

• Weeks 2–3: Training workshops and resource preparation 

• Weeks 3–8: Pilot simulations and scenario testing 

• Weeks 8–10: Evaluation and refinements 

• Weeks 10–14: Documentation and scaling preparation 



**4.3.3 Roles & resources **

• Project lead \(researcher/analyst\): coordinate pilots, validate results, and liaise with stakeholders. 

• System admin / DevOps: host, secure, and maintain the application; manage backups. 

• Trainers \(1–2\): deliver workshops and prepare training materials. 

• Traffic department liaisons \(2–3\): arrange data collection and interpret local constraints. 

• Hardware & software: modern laptop/desktop with GPU optional for visualization, stable internet, cloud instance or on-prem server with recommended CPU/memory based on expected scenario complexity. 





**199 **





**4.3.4 Data, monitoring & evaluation **

• KPIs: mean absolute error between simulated and observed travel times/counts; time to build/run scenario; user satisfaction \(post-pilot survey\); number of scenarios executed per session. 

• Monitoring: usage logs, run durations, memory/cpu metrics. 

• Validation: compare simulator outputs against short field measurements \(e.g., manual counts or short sensor logs\) for each pilot scenario. 

**4.3.5 Maintenance & sustainability **

• Establish a monthly maintenance cadence: backups, dependency updates, and performance profiling. 

• Define a bug triage and feature prioritization process with a small product backlog \(issue tracking\). 

• Budget considerations for hosting, compute scaling, and optional third-party AI services \(if continued\). 



**4.4 Recommendation **

The following recommendations are prioritized by expected impact and implementation effort to strengthen the Traffic Simulator for operational use and future research. 





**200 **





**4.4.1 Short-term \(low effort, high impact\) **

• Add contextual in-app help: tooltips, short embedded screencasts, and a searchable help index mapped to common tasks \(scenario creation, exporting reports\). 

• Provide performance guidance: add “recommended settings” for heavy scenarios \(reduce visual fidelity, limit vehicle density, run at coarse granularity\). 

• Improve recommendation explainability: when the system suggests interventions, include short rationales and the key metrics that triggered the suggestion. 



**4.4.2 Medium-term \(moderate effort\) **

• Implement per-scenario level-of-detail controls and background/queue processing for heavy jobs \(allow long runs to execute asynchronously with notification on completion\). 

• Expand export/reporting templates: one-page executive summaries and templates formatted for municipal reports \(PDF \+ CSV\). 

• Create a pilot validation protocol and instrument \(data logger templates, manual counts, and a standard comparison worksheet\) to systematically compare simulation outputs to field measurements. 





**201 **





**4.4.3 Long-term \(higher effort\) **

• Develop APIs for integration with city traffic data feeds \(e.g., sensors, signal controllers\) to support near-real-time scenario seeding and validation. 

• Add advanced analytics and calibration tools \(parameter tuning, stochastic run aggregation, sensitivity analysis\) to support rigorous comparative evaluation. 

• Consider a mobile/web responsive front-end or a lightweight mobile app for field officers to view scenario summaries and quick visualizations on phones/tablets. 



**4.4.4 Research & evaluation recommendations **

• Conduct a formal field validation study with a larger, domain-representative sample \(n ≥ 30 analysts/officers\) to confirm perceived usability and the empirical accuracy of the simulator’s outputs across diverse contexts. 

• Evaluate the impact of the Simulator in real decision cycles by measuring whether recommended interventions lead to measurable changes in key traffic indicators in short pilot deployments. 

• Publish a technical note describing model assumptions, random seed handling, averaging of multiple runs, and recommended number of replications for robust estimates. 





**202 **





**4.4.5 Operational & governance suggestions **

• Document privacy and data governance practices for any integrated or imported datasets \(OSM extracts, sensor feeds, CCTV counts\). 

• Define an SLA for pilot hosting and a support channel for incident reporting and feature requests. 

• Engage stakeholders early with short, focused policy briefs showing the Simulator’s capability to answer typical decisions \(e.g., evaluate a signal retiming vs. lane reallocation\) and provide an economical plan for wider agency rollout.
